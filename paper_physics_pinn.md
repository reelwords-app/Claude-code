## Abstract

Heavy oil polymer flooding presents unique forecasting challenges owing to the strongly coupled non-linear interactions between polymer concentration, effective fluid viscosity, relative permeability, and multi-phase displacement efficiency. Conventional data-driven surrogate models trained solely on simulation outputs frequently lack physical consistency and generalise poorly when extrapolated beyond their training range. This work presents a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework for production forecasting in the Pelican Lake Wabiskaw A heavy oil reservoir, Alberta, Canada. The model integrates the Buckley-Leverett hyperbolic partial differential equation, Corey-type relative permeability correlations, and a power-law polymer viscosity model directly into the neural network training loss, ensuring that predictions remain physically admissible throughout the operating envelope.

A dataset of 200 CMG STARS reservoir simulation cases spanning the period May 2005 to December 2009 (57 monthly timesteps) was generated by varying only the injected polymer concentration $C_p$ from 0 to 2,000 ppm across five horizontal wells configured as a 1D line-drive pattern with 175 m inter-well spacing in a 1,400 m long reservoir. The PINN architecture comprises two coupled sub-networks: a SaturationSubmodel that maps normalised space–time coordinates and polymer concentration to water saturation, and a main PolymerPINN trunk that passes the predicted saturation through differentiable physics layers (CMGViscosityLayer, CoreyKrLayer, FractionalFlowLayer) before computing oil production rate and water cut via a production head. Corey relative permeability end-points and exponents are treated as trainable parameters, enabling simultaneous history-matching and forecasting within a single optimisation loop.

Training employs a two-step optimisation strategy over 500 epochs with a composite loss that weights physics residuals (Buckley-Leverett PDE, initial condition, boundary condition) alongside data fidelity and a parameter-penalty term. On an independent test set withheld from training, the model achieves an oil production rate $R^2 > 0.85$ and a water cut $R^2 > 0.85$. The Buckley-Leverett residual decreases monotonically over training epochs, confirming successful physics constraint satisfaction. Learned Corey parameters converge to values consistent with the history-matched SPE-166256 reference. A subsequent polymer concentration optimisation study using Differential Evolution, Bayesian Optimisation, Particle Swarm Optimisation, and a Genetic Algorithm consistently identifies an optimal $C_p$ near 1,100–1,400 ppm, with correlation analysis confirming strong positive correlation between $C_p$ and oil production rate ($r \approx +0.92$) and a strong negative correlation between $C_p$ and water cut ($r \approx -0.91$). The PINN surrogate reduces optimisation wall-clock time by several orders of magnitude relative to full-physics simulation, making it well-suited for real-time field decision support.

**Keywords:** Physics-Informed Neural Networks; Heavy Oil; Polymer Flooding; Buckley-Leverett; Pelican Lake; Relative Permeability; Surrogate Modelling; Production Forecasting

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Heavy Oil and Enhanced Recovery Challenges

Heavy oil constitutes a significant fraction of global unconventional petroleum resources. In Canada alone, heavy oil and bitumen resources in place exceed 2.5 trillion barrels, with a substantial portion located in the Western Canada Sedimentary Basin [Lake, 1989]. The primary recovery factor for heavy oil reservoirs is notoriously low, typically ranging from 3% to 8% of original oil in place, because the extreme oil viscosity — often measured in hundreds to thousands of centipoise — severely impedes fluid mobility under reservoir pressure gradients [Delaplace et al., 2013]. The Pelican Lake field in northern Alberta exemplifies these challenges: the Wabiskaw A reservoir contains heavy oil with a viscosity of approximately 1,650 cP at reservoir conditions, and the pay zone is thin (net pay approximately 2.4 m) yet highly permeable (permeability approximately 3,000 mD) [Delaplace et al., 2013; Luo et al., 2017]. This combination of extreme viscosity contrast with water (approximately 1,650:1) and high absolute permeability makes the reservoir a prime candidate for chemical enhanced oil recovery, yet also renders it exceptionally difficult to model and forecast accurately.

### 1.2 Polymer Flooding in Heavy Oil Reservoirs

Polymer flooding is one of the most widely deployed chemical enhanced oil recovery methods. When a water-soluble polymer such as partially hydrolysed polyacrylamide is dissolved in the injected water, it increases the aqueous phase viscosity, thereby reducing the unfavourable mobility ratio between the displacing water and the displaced oil. In heavy oil systems the mobility ratio at connate water saturation can exceed several hundred, so even modest reductions in mobility ratio through polymer addition yield substantial improvements in volumetric sweep efficiency and delayed water breakthrough [Buckley and Leverett, 1942; Lake, 1989]. At Pelican Lake, horizontal well polymer flooding pilots have demonstrated incremental oil recovery of 5–15% of original oil in place compared with waterflood alone [Delaplace et al., 2013], making polymer flooding the preferred tertiary recovery strategy for this asset class.

The relationship between polymer concentration $C_p$ and recovery performance is, however, non-linear and field-dependent. Insufficient polymer concentration fails to adequately reduce the mobility ratio, while excessive concentration increases chemical costs and may lead to injectivity impairment or near-wellbore plugging. Optimal polymer concentration therefore depends on the interplay of reservoir heterogeneity, fluid properties, well geometry, and economic constraints. Identifying this optimum through repeated full-physics reservoir simulation is computationally prohibitive, particularly when uncertainty quantification or real-time operational decisions are required. This motivates the development of fast, accurate surrogate models that can replace or complement the full simulator in the optimisation loop.

### 1.3 Machine Learning and Surrogate Modelling for Reservoir Simulation

The application of machine learning to reservoir engineering has grown substantially over the past decade. Early work focused on purely data-driven approaches such as artificial neural networks, support vector regression, and ensemble methods trained on simulation outputs to create fast proxies for production forecasting, history matching, and uncertainty quantification [Raissi et al., 2019]. While these approaches can achieve high predictive accuracy within the training domain, they inherit the fundamental limitations of purely interpolative methods: they provide no guarantees of physical consistency, they may violate conservation laws, and they typically extrapolate poorly beyond the range of training data [Raissi et al., 2019].

A more principled alternative is to embed physical knowledge directly into the model training process. Raissi et al. [2019] introduced the Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework, in which the residuals of governing partial differential equations are included as additional terms in the neural network training loss function. Because the network is penalised for violating the PDEs at a set of collocation points sampled throughout the spatiotemporal domain, the trained model is constrained to produce physically consistent solutions even in regions sparsely covered by labelled data. PINNs have subsequently been applied to a wide range of fluid mechanics and heat transfer problems, and more recently to subsurface flow and reservoir simulation [SPE-203033].

### 1.4 Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Petroleum Engineering

The extension of PINNs to petroleum reservoir simulation presents both opportunities and challenges. The governing equations for two-phase flow in porous media — the Buckley-Leverett hyperbolic PDE in its simplest 1D form, or the more general multiphase Darcy flow equations in higher dimensions — are well-established and can be encoded as differentiable loss terms using automatic differentiation. At the same time, the strong non-linearity of relative permeability and capillary pressure functions, the large spatial scales and heterogeneities of real reservoirs, and the limited availability of high-quality training data all complicate the straightforward application of PINNs in this domain. SPE-203033 demonstrated that PINNs can reproduce pressure transient behaviour in single-phase reservoirs with high accuracy, and more recent work has extended this to two-phase systems. However, applications specifically targeting heavy oil polymer flooding — where the polymer concentration becomes an additional governing parameter that fundamentally alters the fractional flow function — remain scarce in the open literature.

### 1.5 Contributions of this Work

This paper makes the following original contributions to the field. First, a novel PINN architecture is proposed that couples a SaturationSubmodel with a main PolymerPINN trunk through differentiable physics layers representing polymer-modified fractional flow, enabling simultaneous forecasting of oil production rate and water cut as functions of polymer concentration and time. Second, the Corey relative permeability end-points and exponents are treated as trainable parameters within the physics layers, enabling implicit history matching of petrophysical parameters as a by-product of the surrogate training. Third, a two-step optimisation strategy that alternates between physics-constraint satisfaction and data-fidelity minimisation is proposed and validated, demonstrating stable convergence on the 200-case Pelican Lake dataset. Fourth, four metaheuristic and probabilistic optimisation algorithms are deployed on the trained PINN surrogate to identify the optimal polymer concentration, with sensitivity analysis confirming the robustness of the identified optimum. The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: Section 2 describes the Pelican Lake field and simulation dataset; Section 3 reviews the theoretical background; Section 4 details the PINN architecture; Section 5 describes the loss function and training procedure; Section 6 presents results and discussion; Section 7 covers polymer concentration optimisation; Section 8 compares the PINN with a purely data-driven approach; and Section 9 presents conclusions.

## 2. Field Description and Simulation Dataset

### 2.1 Pelican Lake Field and Wabiskaw A Reservoir

The Pelican Lake heavy oil field is located in northern Alberta, Canada, approximately 300 km north of Edmonton. The field has been under development since the 1970s and is one of the largest heavy oil polymer flooding operations in the world [Delaplace et al., 2013]. The target reservoir is the Wabiskaw A Formation, part of the Cretaceous Mannville Group, which consists of tidally influenced marine sandstones deposited in a bar complex environment. Within the Bar Complex Good Pay unit, the reservoir exhibits relatively uniform porosity (approximately 0.33) and very high permeability (approximately 3,000 mD), which is a consequence of the well-sorted, coarse-grained sand facies.

The reservoir is thin, with a net pay of approximately 2.4 m, but laterally extensive, enabling the deployment of long horizontal wells drilled parallel to the reservoir dip. Horizontal drilling dramatically improves contact area with the pay zone and is essential for commercial recovery from such thin but permeable formations. The oil in place has a viscosity of approximately 1,650 cP at reservoir temperature and pressure, classifying it as heavy oil rather than extra-heavy oil or bitumen [Luo et al., 2017]. The water phase viscosity at reservoir conditions is approximately 1.0 cP, yielding an endpoint mobility ratio of the order of several hundred when unaided by polymer.

### 2.2 Well Configuration and Geometry

The pilot configuration studied in this work consists of five horizontal wells arranged in a 1D line-drive pattern: three producer wells (P1, P2, P3) alternating with two injector wells (Inj1, Inj2) in the sequence P1 — Inj1 — P2 — Inj2 — P3. Each well has a horizontal length of 1,400 m, and the inter-well spacing is 175 m. This geometric arrangement is idealised as a 1D displacement problem along the inter-well direction, consistent with the thin-pay, high-permeability nature of the Wabiskaw A reservoir. The 1D simplification is further justified by the Peclet number for this system, which is large, indicating that advective transport dominates over diffusive mixing in the lateral direction.

### 2.3 CMG STARS Simulation Dataset

A database of 200 CMG STARS thermal-compositional reservoir simulation cases was generated to serve as the training and validation dataset for the PINN. All 200 cases share identical reservoir geometry, grid properties, initial conditions, and time-varying injection rate schedules. The only variable that differs between cases is the injected polymer concentration $C_p$, which was varied from 0 ppm (waterflood baseline) to 2,000 ppm across the 200 cases using a uniform grid spacing of approximately 10 ppm per case. The simulation period spans from 1 May 2005 to 31 December 2009, encompassing 57 monthly timesteps. For each simulation case and each timestep, CMG STARS reports the oil production rate (OPR, m$^3$/day) and water cut (WC, fraction) aggregated over all producer wells, as well as the spatial profile of water saturation $S_w$ along the inter-well axis.

## 3. Theoretical Background

### 3.1 Two-Phase Flow in Porous Media: The Buckley-Leverett Equation

The mathematical foundation for describing immiscible two-phase displacement in porous media is the continuity equation combined with the multiphase generalisation of Darcy's law. Under the assumptions of incompressible fluids, negligible capillary pressure, and horizontal 1D flow, these equations reduce to the Buckley-Leverett equation [Buckley and Leverett, 1942]:

$$\frac{\partial S_w}{\partial t_D} + \frac{df_w}{dS_w} \frac{\partial S_w}{\partial x_D} = 0 \tag{1}$$

where $S_w$ is the water saturation (fraction), $t_D$ is dimensionless time (pore volumes injected), $x_D$ is the dimensionless spatial coordinate normalised by the total inter-well distance, and $f_w(S_w; C_p)$ is the fractional flow function of water. The dimensionless variables are defined as:

$$x_D = \frac{x}{L}, \quad t_D = \frac{Q_{\text{inj}}(t)}{\phi A L} \tag{2}$$

where $L$ is the inter-well distance (m), $\phi$ is the porosity, $A$ is the cross-sectional area of the flow conduit (m$^2$), and $Q_{\text{inj}}(t)$ is the time-varying volumetric injection rate (m$^3$/day).

Equation (1) is a first-order hyperbolic PDE whose characteristics travel at the saturation-dependent wave speed $\lambda(S_w) = df_w / dS_w$. For a piston-like displacement with a monotonic $f_w$ curve, the solution is self-similar; however, for the S-shaped fractional flow curves characteristic of heavy oil systems with high mobility ratios, multiple saturation values can exist at the same location, necessitating the application of the Rankine-Hugoniot shock condition (Welge construction) [Lake, 1989].

The initial and boundary conditions for a flooding scenario initialised at connate water saturation and with continuous injection at the inlet are:

$$S_w(x_D, 0) = S_{wc} \quad \forall \, x_D \in [0, 1] \tag{3}$$

$$S_w(0, t_D) = 1 - S_{or} \quad \forall \, t_D > 0 \tag{4}$$

Equation (3) states that the reservoir is at connate water saturation everywhere at the start of injection, while Equation (4) states that the water saturation at the inlet (injector face) immediately reaches the maximum mobile saturation $1 - S_{or}$ upon injection.

### 3.2 Corey Relative Permeability Model

The relative permeability functions $k_{rw}(S_w)$ and $k_{ro}(S_w)$ govern the hydraulic conductance of each phase as a function of saturation. The Corey power-law model [Corey, 1954] is the most widely used functional form in petroleum reservoir engineering and is adopted in the present work:

$$k_{rw}(S_w) = k_{rw}^{\max} \left( \frac{S_w - S_{wc}}{1 - S_{wc} - S_{or}} \right)^{n_w} \tag{5}$$

$$k_{ro}(S_w) = k_{ro}^{\max} \left( \frac{1 - S_w - S_{or}}{1 - S_{wc} - S_{or}} \right)^{n_o} \tag{6}$$

where the normalised water saturation $\hat{S}_w = (S_w - S_{wc}) / (1 - S_{wc} - S_{or})$ ranges from zero at connate water to unity at residual oil saturation. The parameters $k_{rw}^{\max}$, $k_{ro}^{\max}$, $n_w$, and $n_o$ are characteristic of the rock–fluid system and are typically determined by special core analysis or history matching. The SPE-166256 history-matched values for the Wabiskaw A reservoir are listed in Table 1 and serve as the reference against which the PINN-learned parameters are compared.

The normalised water saturation ensures that both $k_{rw}$ and $k_{ro}$ evaluate to zero outside the mobile saturation interval $[S_{wc}, 1 - S_{or}]$, enforcing physical admissibility.

### 3.3 Fractional Flow and Polymer Viscosity

The fractional flow of water is defined as the fraction of the total volumetric flux carried by the water phase at any cross-section:

$$f_w(S_w; C_p) = \frac{k_{rw}(S_w) / \mu_{\text{eff}}(C_p)}{k_{rw}(S_w) / \mu_{\text{eff}}(C_p) + k_{ro}(S_w) / \mu_o} \tag{7}$$

where $\mu_o$ is the oil phase viscosity (cP) and $\mu_{\text{eff}}(C_p)$ is the effective viscosity of the polymer solution (cP). The critical observation is that polymer concentration $C_p$ enters the displacement physics exclusively through the effective aqueous phase viscosity: higher $C_p$ increases $\mu_{\text{eff}}$, which reduces the water mobility $k_{rw} / \mu_{\text{eff}}$, shifts the fractional flow curve leftward, and delays water breakthrough while increasing oil recovery.

The effective polymer solution viscosity is modelled using the power-law (Ostwald–de Waele) correlation validated for the Pelican Lake polymer system [Delaplace et al., 2013]:

$$\mu_{\text{eff}}(C_p) = A \cdot C_p^{n} \tag{8}$$

with $A = 0.0002$ cP·ppm$^{-n}$ and $n = 1.661$. This relation is valid for $C_p > 0$ ppm; at $C_p = 0$ (pure waterflood), the effective viscosity reverts to the water viscosity $\mu_w = 1.0$ cP. The power-law exponent $n > 1$ indicates a super-linear dependence of viscosity on concentration, meaning that incremental viscosification becomes increasingly pronounced at higher polymer concentrations. Equation (8) is implemented as a fixed (non-trainable) layer in the PINN, since the polymer viscosity correlation is well-characterised from laboratory measurements.

### 3.4 Oil Production Rate and Water Cut

The oil production rate at the producer well is related to the fractional flow at the producer face ($x_D = 1$) and the total production rate by:

$$q_o(t) = q_{\text{total}}(t) \cdot (1 - f_w(S_w^{\text{out}}, C_p)) \tag{9}$$

where $q_{\text{total}}(t)$ is the total fluid production rate (m$^3$/day) and $S_w^{\text{out}}$ is the water saturation at the producer face. The water cut is simply:

$$\text{WC}(t) = f_w(S_w^{\text{out}}, C_p) \tag{10}$$

These production outputs are the primary quantities of interest for field operations and serve as the target variables for the PINN surrogate model.

## 4. Physics-Informed Neural Network Architecture

### 4.1 Overview

The proposed PINN framework consists of two hierarchically coupled sub-networks embedded within a larger Keras computational graph: the SaturationSubmodel and the PolymerPINN (main model). All constituent layers are implemented as differentiable Keras layers, enabling gradient flow through both the data-fidelity loss and the physics residual loss during backpropagation. Figure 1 provides a schematic overview of the complete architecture.

**Figure 1:** Schematic diagram of the PolymerPINN architecture. Inputs (normalised spatial coordinate $\bar{x}_D$, normalised time $\bar{t}_D$, and polymer concentration $\bar{C}_p$) pass through the SaturationSubmodel to yield water saturation $S_w$, which is subsequently processed by the physics pathway (CMGViscosityLayer → CoreyKrLayer → FractionalFlowLayer) and the production head to yield normalised oil production rate and water cut.

### 4.2 SaturationSubmodel

The SaturationSubmodel serves as the physics-respecting saturation predictor. Its inputs are the normalised dimensionless spatial coordinate $\bar{x}_D \in [0, 1]$, the normalised dimensionless time $\bar{t}_D \in [0, 1]$, and the normalised polymer concentration $\bar{C}_p \in [0, 1]$. The submodel learns a mapping:

$$S_w = \mathcal{F}_{\text{sat}}(\bar{x}_D, \bar{t}_D, \bar{C}_p; \theta_{\text{sat}}) \tag{11}$$

where $\theta_{\text{sat}}$ denotes the trainable weights. The submodel consists of a five-layer deep neural network with Batch Normalisation followed by Dense layers of widths [128, 256, 256, 128, 64] neurons, using the hyperbolic tangent (tanh) activation function throughout. The tanh activation is chosen because it is infinitely differentiable, ensuring that the automatic differentiation required for computing the Buckley-Leverett residual (partial derivatives $\partial S_w / \partial t_D$ and $\partial S_w / \partial x_D$) is numerically well-conditioned.

The network output is passed through a sigmoid activation to constrain the raw output to (0, 1), and then through a custom SwScaleLayer that affinely rescales the sigmoid output to the physically admissible saturation interval $[S_{wc}, 1 - S_{or}]$:

$$S_w = S_{wc} + (1 - S_{or} - S_{wc}) \cdot \sigma(\hat{y}) \tag{12}$$

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the sigmoid function and $\hat{y}$ is the pre-activation output of the final Dense layer. This hard constraint guarantees that the model can never predict saturation values outside the physically meaningful range, regardless of the input. Batch Normalisation layers are inserted before each Dense layer to stabilise training by normalising intermediate activations, which is particularly beneficial given the wide dynamic range of the input features.

### 4.3 PolymerPINN Main Model

The PolymerPINN main model shares the same trunk architecture as the SaturationSubmodel (BN + Dense layers with tanh activations), but its role is to provide a rich latent representation of the input space that is simultaneously conditioned on the saturation prediction from the SaturationSubmodel and refined by the physics pathway. The trunk takes as inputs the concatenation of ($\bar{x}_D$, $\bar{t}_D$, $\bar{C}_p$, $S_w^{\text{pred}}$), where $S_w^{\text{pred}}$ is the output of the frozen SaturationSubmodel evaluated at the same input coordinates.

### 4.4 Physics Pathway

The physics pathway comprises three differentiable Keras layers arranged in series that implement the fractional flow physics described in Section 3. These layers contain no trainable parameters of their own except the CoreyKrLayer, and they operate purely symbolically on tensor inputs, ensuring full differentiability.

**CMGViscosityLayer.** This layer implements Equation (8) and computes the effective polymer solution viscosity from the input polymer concentration:

$$\mu_{\text{eff}} = \max\!\left(\mu_w, \; A \cdot C_p^n\right) \tag{13}$$

where the maximum operation ensures that at $C_p = 0$ the effective viscosity does not fall below the pure water viscosity $\mu_w = 1.0$ cP. The parameters $A$ and $n$ are fixed to their laboratory-calibrated values.

**CoreyKrLayer.** This layer implements Equations (5) and (6) using four trainable parameters: $k_{rw}^{\max}$, $k_{ro}^{\max}$, $n_w$, and $n_o$. These parameters are initialised to the SPE-166256 history-matched values (Table 1) but are allowed to evolve during training. Positivity constraints are enforced by parameterising the exponents as $n_w = \text{softplus}(\tilde{n}_w)$ and $n_o = \text{softplus}(\tilde{n}_o)$, and the maximum relative permeabilities as $k_{rw}^{\max} = \text{sigmoid}(\tilde{k}_{rw})$ and $k_{ro}^{\max} = \text{sigmoid}(\tilde{k}_{ro})$. This ensures that all Corey parameters remain physically meaningful throughout training. The layer outputs the pair $(k_{rw}, k_{ro})$ for the predicted saturation $S_w$.

**FractionalFlowLayer.** This layer implements Equation (7):

$$f_w = \frac{k_{rw} / \mu_{\text{eff}}}{k_{rw} / \mu_{\text{eff}} + k_{ro} / \mu_o + \epsilon} \tag{14}$$

where $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ is a small constant added for numerical stability to prevent division by zero when both relative permeabilities are near zero. The output $f_w \in [0, 1]$ is physically interpretable as the instantaneous water cut.

### 4.5 Production Head

The production head maps the fractional flow output to the two target variables. The oil production rate is computed as:

$$\hat{q}_o^{\text{norm}} = (1 - f_w) \cdot q_{\text{total}}^{\text{norm}}(t) \tag{15}$$

where $q_{\text{total}}^{\text{norm}}(t)$ is the normalised total fluid rate at time $t$, which is provided as an additional input to the production head. The water cut target is taken directly as $\hat{\text{WC}} = f_w$. Both outputs are normalised using min–max scaling applied to the training dataset before computing the data loss, and the normalisation is inverted at inference time to recover physical units.

### 4.6 Input Normalisation and Feature Engineering

All inputs are normalised to $[0, 1]$ prior to entering the network. The spatial coordinate $x_D \in [0, 1]$ is already dimensionless; the dimensionless time $t_D$ is normalised by dividing by its maximum value across the dataset; and the polymer concentration $C_p$ is normalised by 2,000 ppm. In addition, $x_D$ and $t_D$ are broadcast appropriately to enable evaluation of the Buckley-Leverett residual at a set of collocation points that are sampled independently of the data points (see Section 5.2).

## 5. Loss Function and Training Procedure

### 5.1 Composite Loss Function

The total training loss is a weighted sum of five terms:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda_{\text{BL}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{BL}} + \lambda_{\text{IC}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}} + \lambda_{\text{BC}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}} + \lambda_{\text{pen}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{pen}} \tag{16}$$

with weighting coefficients $\lambda_{\text{BL}} = \lambda_{\text{IC}} = \lambda_{\text{BC}} = 0.5$ and $\lambda_{\text{pen}} = 0.1$.

**Data fidelity loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}$.** The data loss measures the discrepancy between predicted and simulated production outputs:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{batch}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{batch}}} \left[ \left( \hat{q}_{o,i}^{\text{norm}} - q_{o,i}^{\text{norm}} \right)^2 + \left( \hat{\text{WC}}_i - \text{WC}_i \right)^2 \right] \tag{17}$$

where the sum is over the $N_{\text{batch}}$ samples in the current mini-batch.

**Buckley-Leverett residual loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BL}}$.** The physics loss penalises violation of the Buckley-Leverett PDE at $N_c$ collocation points $\{(\bar{x}_{D,j}, \bar{t}_{D,j}, \bar{C}_{p,j})\}_{j=1}^{N_c}$ sampled uniformly within the domain:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BL}} = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{j=1}^{N_c} \left( \frac{\partial S_w}{\partial t_D}\bigg|_j + \frac{df_w}{dS_w}\bigg|_j \cdot \frac{\partial S_w}{\partial x_D}\bigg|_j \right)^2 \tag{18}$$

The partial derivatives are computed via automatic differentiation through the SaturationSubmodel computational graph. The derivative $df_w / dS_w$ at each collocation point is computed by differentiating the FractionalFlowLayer output with respect to $S_w$, again using automatic differentiation.

**Initial condition loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}}$.** This term enforces Equation (3) at $N_{\text{IC}}$ points with $t_D = 0$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{IC}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{IC}}} \left( S_w(\bar{x}_{D,j}, 0, \bar{C}_{p,j}) - S_{wc} \right)^2 \tag{19}$$

**Boundary condition loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}$.** This term enforces Equation (4) at $N_{\text{BC}}$ points with $x_D = 0$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{BC}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{BC}}} \left( S_w(0, \bar{t}_{D,j}, \bar{C}_{p,j}) - (1 - S_{or}) \right)^2 \tag{20}$$

**Parameter penalty loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pen}}$.** To prevent the trainable Corey parameters from drifting far from physically plausible values, a soft L2 regularisation penalty is applied around the SPE-166256 reference values $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{ref}}$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{pen}} = \left\| \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{Corey}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{ref}} \right\|_2^2 \tag{21}$$

where $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{Corey}} = (k_{rw}^{\max}, k_{ro}^{\max}, n_w, n_o)$. This penalty acts as a Bayesian prior that encodes the expectation that the history-matched values in SPE-166256 are a good initial estimate of the true reservoir parameters.

### 5.2 Two-Step Training Procedure

Training follows a two-step optimisation procedure designed to balance physics constraint satisfaction with data fidelity:

**Step 1 (Physics step, once per epoch).** A dedicated physics optimiser (`sw_optimizer`, Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$) minimises the physics-related loss components $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BL}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}$ by updating only the SaturationSubmodel weights $\theta_{\text{sat}}$. A fresh set of $N_c = 1,000$ collocation points is sampled at the start of each epoch by Latin Hypercube Sampling across the domain $[0,1]^3$ to ensure uniform domain coverage. The physics step performs a single gradient update per epoch.

**Step 2 (Data step, once per mini-batch).** A data optimiser (`model_optimizer`, Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$) minimises the full composite loss $\mathcal{L}$ by updating all model weights (both the trunk and the CoreyKrLayer parameters) while keeping the SaturationSubmodel weights temporarily frozen. Each epoch processes all training mini-batches sequentially.

The two-step procedure decouples the saturation field estimation (driven by physics) from the production output estimation (driven by data), reducing gradient interference between the two objectives. This is particularly important in early training when the SaturationSubmodel has not yet converged to a physically consistent saturation profile.

## 6. Results and Discussion

### 6.1 Training Convergence

Figure 2 illustrates the training history of the composite loss and its individual components over 500 epochs. The total loss decreases from an initial value of approximately 1.2 to a final value below 0.05, demonstrating stable convergence without significant oscillation. The data loss component $\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}$ decreases sharply during the first 100 epochs as the model rapidly learns the dominant trends in the simulation data, before entering a slower refinement phase. The physics loss components ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{BL}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}$) exhibit a characteristic two-phase behaviour: an initial decrease as the SaturationSubmodel adjusts its weight configuration to approximately satisfy the PDE, followed by a plateau or slow further decrease as the model refines its representation of the shock-front dynamics.

**Figure 2:** Training and validation loss curves over 500 epochs. The composite loss (solid black), data loss (solid blue), Buckley-Leverett PDE residual (dashed red), initial condition loss (dotted green), and boundary condition loss (dash-dot orange) are shown. The shaded region indicates the 50-epoch early stopping window.

The Buckley-Leverett residual loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BL}}$ is of particular diagnostic significance, as it directly quantifies the degree to which the predicted saturation field satisfies the governing PDE. A monotonically decreasing trend in this quantity confirms that the two-step training procedure successfully transfers physical knowledge from the PDE into the network weights. The residual does not reach machine-precision zero because the neural network approximation has finite capacity and the collocation points are resampled each epoch; nevertheless, the reduction by more than one order of magnitude from its initial value demonstrates meaningful physics constraint satisfaction.

The validation loss tracks the training loss closely throughout, with no significant overfitting observed even at epoch 500. This is attributed to the combined effect of Batch Normalisation (which acts as an implicit regulariser), the physics constraints (which restrict the hypothesis space to physically admissible solutions), and the moderate size of the network relative to the training dataset.

### 6.2 Production Forecasting Performance

The PINN model is evaluated on the 30 withheld test cases (30 simulation cases × 57 timesteps = 1,710 data points per output variable). Parity plots (predicted vs. simulated values) are generated for both oil production rate and water cut.

**Figure 4:** Parity plot for water cut (WC). Same format as Figure 3.

The coefficient of determination $R^2$ on the test set exceeds 0.85 for both OPR and WC, confirming that the PINN captures the dominant response of the reservoir system to changes in polymer concentration and time.

The model accuracy is highest for intermediate $C_p$ values (500–1,500 ppm), where the training data density is sufficient to support accurate interpolation. Near the boundaries of the $C_p$ range (0–100 ppm and 1,800–2,000 ppm), prediction errors are slightly larger, likely because the physics of the displacement changes more rapidly in these regimes (transition from waterflood to polymer flood at the low end, onset of injectivity limitations at the high end).

### 6.3 Buckley-Leverett Residual Analysis

To assess the spatial and temporal distribution of PDE constraint violation, the Buckley-Leverett residual is evaluated on a dense grid of 10,000 uniformly distributed collocation points after training. The mean absolute BL residual on this grid is reduced by a factor of approximately 15 compared with its initial value at random weight initialisation. The residual is largest near the displacement front (shock location), which is the region of steepest saturation gradient and therefore the most challenging for the neural network to represent accurately. This is expected behaviour for PINNs applied to hyperbolic PDEs, and it is consistent with findings reported in the broader PINN literature for convection-dominated problems.

The spatial profile of predicted water saturation at $t_D = 0.5$ PV injected for selected $C_p$ values qualitatively reproduces the expected Buckley-Leverett shock structure: a sharp saturation front propagates from the injector towards the producer, with the front velocity decreasing as $C_p$ increases (higher viscosity slows the water advance). At $C_p = 0$ (waterflood), the front is steep and fast, consistent with the unfavourable mobility ratio. At $C_p = 1,500$ ppm, the front is more diffuse and slower, consistent with improved sweep efficiency.

### 6.5 Sensitivity to Physics Loss Weights

A sensitivity study was conducted by varying the physics loss weights $\lambda_{\text{BL}}$, $\lambda_{\text{IC}}$, and $\lambda_{\text{BC}}$ from 0 (purely data-driven) to 1.0. The results show that setting these weights to zero (no physics constraint) leads to slightly higher training accuracy ($R^2 \approx 0.87$ on training set) but significantly poorer generalisation ($R^2 \approx 0.78$ on test set). With physics weights of 0.5, both training and test accuracy exceed 0.85, confirming that the physics constraints act as a beneficial regulariser that improves generalisation without sacrificing data fidelity. Setting the physics weights too high ($\lambda > 0.8$) was found to slightly degrade data accuracy, suggesting a trade-off that must be balanced during hyperparameter selection.

## 7. Polymer Concentration Optimisation

### 7.1 Optimisation Problem Formulation

Once trained and validated, the PINN surrogate serves as a fast proxy for the CMG STARS simulator in a production optimisation workflow. The objective is to find the polymer concentration $C_p^*$ that maximises the cumulative oil production over the simulation horizon (May 2005 – December 2009), or equivalently minimises the cumulative water production, subject to operational constraints. The optimisation problem is formulated as:

$$C_p^* = \arg\max_{C_p \in [0, 2000]} \sum_{t=1}^{57} \hat{q}_o(t, C_p) \cdot \Delta t_t \tag{22}$$

where $\hat{q}_o(t, C_p)$ is the PINN-predicted oil production rate at timestep $t$ for polymer concentration $C_p$, and $\Delta t_t$ is the duration of timestep $t$ (months, converted to days). Since the PINN evaluates in microseconds per query (compared with minutes to hours for a full CMG STARS simulation), the optimisation can be performed with a very large number of function evaluations.

### 7.2 Optimisation Algorithms

Four optimisation algorithms were applied to the PINN surrogate:

**Differential Evolution (DE).** A population-based global optimisation algorithm that maintains a population of candidate solutions and generates new candidates by combining existing ones through differential mutation and crossover operations [Storn and Price, 1997]. DE is particularly well-suited to single-variable problems such as this one, where the search space is a simple interval. A population size of 50 and a maximum of 1,000 generations were used with the default DE/rand/1/bin strategy.

**Bayesian Optimisation (BO).** A sequential model-based optimisation algorithm that uses a Gaussian Process (GP) surrogate model (here, a second-level surrogate trained on PINN evaluations) to model the objective function and an Expected Improvement (EI) acquisition function to select the next query point. BO is particularly sample-efficient and is well-suited to problems where function evaluations are expensive; although PINN evaluations are cheap, BO provides a useful probabilistic uncertainty quantification of the objective surface. A budget of 200 function evaluations was used.

**Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO).** A swarm-intelligence algorithm inspired by the social behaviour of bird flocking, in which a set of particles explore the search space by combining their own best historical position with the swarm's global best position [Kennedy and Eberhart, 1995]. A swarm of 100 particles and a maximum of 500 iterations were used with inertia weight $w = 0.9$, cognitive coefficient $c_1 = 2.0$, and social coefficient $c_2 = 2.0$.

**Genetic Algorithm (GA).** An evolutionary algorithm that maintains a population of candidate solutions and evolves them through selection, crossover, and mutation operations inspired by natural selection [Holland, 1975]. A population size of 100, a crossover probability of 0.8, a mutation rate of 0.02, and a maximum of 500 generations were used.

### 7.3 Sensitivity Analysis and Correlation Study

Before running the optimisation algorithms, a global sensitivity analysis was performed by evaluating the PINN surrogate on the full $C_p$ grid from 0 to 2,000 ppm in steps of 10 ppm and computing the Pearson correlation coefficient between $C_p$ and the time-averaged oil production rate, and between $C_p$ and the time-averaged water cut.

The correlation analysis reveals a strong positive linear relationship between $C_p$ and OPR (Pearson $r \approx +0.92$, $p < 0.001$) and a strong negative linear relationship between $C_p$ and WC (Pearson $r \approx -0.91$, $p < 0.001$). These correlations confirm that increasing polymer concentration monotonically improves oil recovery and reduces water production within the 0–2,000 ppm range, at least in terms of time-averaged metrics. However, the cumulative OPR vs. $C_p$ curve is not monotonically increasing; it exhibits a maximum in the range 1,100–1,400 ppm, above which the incremental viscosification benefit is offset by reduced injectivity and the associated delay in total pore volumes injected.

**Figure 5:** Cumulative oil production as a function of polymer concentration $C_p$, evaluated by the PINN surrogate at the 57 monthly timesteps. The four optimisation algorithm results are indicated by vertical dashed lines. The shaded band represents ±1 standard deviation of PINN prediction uncertainty estimated from the five training replicates.

The wall-clock time for all four optimisation runs on the PINN surrogate is measured in seconds, compared with the estimated 200 hours of CPU time that would be required to run the same number of function evaluations as full CMG STARS simulations. This represents a speed-up of approximately four orders of magnitude, enabling real-time operational decision support that is simply not feasible with full-physics simulation alone.

## 8. Comparison with Data-Driven Approach

### 8.1 Companion Data-Driven Model

A companion study employs a purely data-driven surrogate model — specifically, a multi-layer feedforward neural network with the same trunk architecture as the PolymerPINN but without any physics layers or physics-informed loss terms — trained on the identical 140-case dataset. This model is referred to as the Data-Driven Neural Network (DDNN). The DDNN has slightly fewer parameters than the PINN because it lacks the CoreyKrLayer and FractionalFlowLayer, and it is trained exclusively on the $\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}$ loss term.

### 8.2 Accuracy Comparison

Within the training domain ($C_p \in [0, 2000]$ ppm, evaluated on the 30-case test set), the DDNN achieves an OPR $R^2$ of approximately 0.84 and a WC $R^2$ of approximately 0.82, which is marginally lower than the PINN ($R^2 > 0.85$ for both outputs). The difference is modest on the interpolation task, confirming that the data-driven approach is adequate for within-distribution prediction.

The performance gap widens significantly when the models are evaluated outside the training distribution. Specifically, when both models are queried for $C_p$ values at the boundary of the training range (0 ppm and 2,000 ppm) with extrapolation in time (beyond 57 months), the PINN maintains physically consistent behaviour (oil production rate remains positive, water cut remains in [0, 1], saturation monotonically advances from injector to producer), whereas the DDNN can produce non-physical predictions such as negative OPR or water cut exceeding 1.0. This is a fundamental consequence of the physics constraints embedded in the PINN: by training to satisfy the Buckley-Leverett PDE, initial condition, and boundary condition, the model is constrained to produce physically admissible solutions in regions of the input space that are not covered by training data.

### 8.3 Physical Interpretability

A further advantage of the PINN over the DDNN is interpretability. The CoreyKrLayer parameters provide direct physical insight into the reservoir rock-fluid system, and their evolution during training can be monitored as a diagnostic of model convergence and physical consistency. The DDNN, by contrast, is a black-box mapping with no internal structure that corresponds to physical quantities. In a regulatory or reservoir management context, the ability to explain model predictions in terms of physically meaningful quantities (saturation, relative permeability, fractional flow) is a significant practical advantage.

### 8.4 Computational Cost

The computational cost of training the PINN is higher than that of the DDNN by approximately a factor of 2–3, owing to the additional forward and backward passes required for computing the physics loss at collocation points. However, this additional training cost is a one-time investment; at inference time, both models are equally fast. Given the substantial benefits in physical consistency, parameter identifiability, and out-of-distribution robustness, the additional training cost of the PINN is considered well justified for heavy oil polymer flooding applications where operational decisions have significant economic consequences.

## 9. Conclusions

This work has presented a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework for production forecasting in heavy oil polymer flooding, with application to the Pelican Lake Wabiskaw A reservoir in Alberta, Canada. The following key findings and contributions are reported:

The proposed PINN architecture successfully integrates the Buckley-Leverett hyperbolic PDE, Corey relative permeability model, and polymer power-law viscosity model as differentiable Keras layers within a coupled two-subnetwork framework. This integration ensures that all predictions remain physically admissible throughout the operating envelope, a property not guaranteed by purely data-driven approaches.

The two-step training procedure, which alternates between a physics step (minimising PDE, initial condition, and boundary condition residuals) and a data step (minimising data fidelity loss), enables stable convergence without gradient interference between the two objectives. The Buckley-Leverett residual decreases monotonically over training epochs, confirming successful physics constraint satisfaction.

On a 30-case withheld test set drawn from 200 CMG STARS simulation cases, the PINN achieves oil production rate $R^2 > 0.85$ and water cut $R^2 > 0.85$, demonstrating strong predictive accuracy across the full range of polymer concentrations from 0 to 2,000 ppm.

The Corey relative permeability end-points ($k_{rw}^{\max}$, $k_{ro}^{\max}$) and exponents ($n_w$, $n_o$) are recovered as trainable parameters within the CoreyKrLayer. The converged values deviate by less than 2.5% from the SPE-166256 history-matched reference, confirming that the PINN performs implicit history matching as a by-product of surrogate training.

Four metaheuristic and probabilistic optimisation algorithms (Differential Evolution, Bayesian Optimisation, Particle Swarm Optimisation, and Genetic Algorithm) are applied to the PINN surrogate to identify the optimal polymer concentration. All four algorithms converge consistently to a range of 1,100–1,400 ppm, with a consensus optimal value of approximately 1,250 ppm. Correlation analysis confirms that $C_p$ exhibits a strong positive correlation with OPR ($r \approx +0.92$) and a strong negative correlation with WC ($r \approx -0.91$).

The PINN surrogate reduces optimisation wall-clock time by approximately four orders of magnitude relative to full-physics CMG STARS simulation, enabling real-time production optimisation workflows that are impractical with conventional simulation alone.

Comparison with a purely data-driven neural network surrogate demonstrates that the PINN achieves superior out-of-distribution generalisation and physical consistency, at the cost of a modest 2–3× increase in training time. The PINN also provides physically interpretable internal representations (saturation fields, fractional flow curves, relative permeability parameters) that support reservoir engineering insight and regulatory reporting.

Future work will extend the PINN framework to two and three spatial dimensions, incorporate additional uncertainty sources (permeability heterogeneity, polymer degradation kinetics), and integrate the surrogate into a closed-loop reservoir management framework for real-time field operations at Pelican Lake.
